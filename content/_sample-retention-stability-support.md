# Retention, Stability and Support

These three terms describe distinct resistance directions and are commonly confused.

## Support

Support is resistance to **vertical forces directed towards the tissues** — the seating force of
occlusal load. It is provided by the denture-bearing area, so support depends on the area, quality
and contour of the tissues beneath the denture base.

Broad coverage distributes load over the widest possible area, which reduces the load per unit area
on the underlying bone. This is the reasoning behind extending a denture base to its functional
limits rather than keeping it small for comfort.

## Stability

Stability is resistance to **horizontal and rotational displacement** during function. A denture may
be well retained at rest and still be unstable when the patient chews or speaks.

Stability is influenced by:

- The height and parallelism of the residual ridge
- Correct tooth position relative to the neutral zone
- Accurate, harmonious occlusion
- A polished surface shaped so cheeks and tongue seat the denture rather than displace it

## Retention

Retention is resistance to **vertical dislodging forces directed away from the tissues**. It relies
on several physical mechanisms acting together rather than on any single factor.

The principal factors are:

- **Interfacial surface tension and viscosity** of the saliva film between base and mucosa
- **Peripheral seal**, which prevents air entering and breaking that film
- **Atmospheric pressure**, which acts only while the peripheral seal remains intact
- **Adhesion** between saliva and both the denture base and the mucosa
- **Cohesion** within the saliva layer itself

Because the seal is what allows atmospheric pressure to contribute at all, an underextended or
overextended border is one of the most common causes of poor retention.

# The Neutral Zone

The neutral zone is the potential space where the displacing forces of the **tongue** acting
outwards are neutralised by the forces of the **cheeks and lips** acting inwards.

Artificial teeth placed inside this zone are subject to balanced muscular forces during function.
Teeth placed outside it are repeatedly displaced by the surrounding musculature, which is why a
technically accurate denture can still be unstable if tooth position ignores the neutral zone.

This concept is particularly valuable in cases with severely resorbed mandibular ridges, where
mechanical retention from the ridge form itself is minimal and muscular control becomes the dominant
stabilising influence.
